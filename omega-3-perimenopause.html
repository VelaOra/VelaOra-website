You're taking fish oil. The bottle says omega-3. You've been taking it for months and you're not sure it's doing anything. There's a reason for that — and it has nothing to do with whether omega-3s work. It has everything to do with what's actually in your bottle.

The evidence for omega-3s in perimenopause is genuinely strong: cardiovascular protection as oestrogen declines, mood regulation through EPA's effects on serotonin pathways, joint inflammation reduction, and support for the skin and eye membrane dryness so many women experience. The problem isn't the science — it's the supplement market.

The Quality Problem Nobody Talks About

Fish oil oxidises. Rancid fish oil not only doesn't work — research suggests it may actively contribute to the inflammatory processes it's supposed to reduce. A significant proportion of fish oils on the market, particularly at lower price points, are oxidised before you open the bottle.

A 2015 study published in Scientific Reports tested 171 fish oil supplements from New Zealand and found that over 80% exceeded recommended oxidation thresholds. This is not an isolated finding — similar results have been replicated in Norwegian, US, and South African markets. The problem is industry-wide.

The smell test is crude but useful: fresh, quality fish oil should smell neutral or faintly of lemon. A distinctly fishy or "off" smell is oxidation. If your capsules smell unpleasant when you cut one open, they're almost certainly rancid.

Why Form Matters — TG vs EE

Most concentrated fish oil capsules are in ethyl ester (EE) form — a processed form that's cheap to manufacture. The problem is absorption. Multiple studies, including research by Dyerberg et al. published in Prostaglandins, Leukotrienes and Essential Fatty Acids, have found that triglyceride (TG) form omega-3 absorbs approximately 70% more effectively than EE form in humans.

This matters practically: you could be taking a high-dose EE supplement and absorbing a fraction of what the label claims, while a lower-dose TG product delivers more to your tissues. Look for "re-esterified triglycerides," "natural triglyceride form," or "rTG" on the label. If the label doesn't specify, it's almost certainly EE.

What the Research Actually Shows

The VITAL trial (Manson et al., NEJM 2019) — one of the largest randomised controlled trials of omega-3 supplementation, following over 25,000 participants — found significant cardiovascular benefits, including a 28% reduction in heart attack risk in people who don't regularly eat fish. For perimenopausal women, whose cardiovascular risk rises as oestrogen declines, this is directly relevant.

On mood: a 2022 Cochrane systematic review found that EPA specifically — not DHA — shows meaningful benefit for depressive symptoms. The ratio in your supplement matters. For mood support, look for EPA-dominant formulas. For general cardiovascular and anti-inflammatory support, a balanced ratio is fine.

On dosing: most research showing benefit uses 1,000–2,000mg of combined EPA+DHA daily. Check the EPA+DHA content on your label — not the total fish oil dose, which is a larger number and largely meaningless.

References: VITAL Trial (Manson et al., NEJM 2019); Cochrane Review EPA (2022); Dyerberg et al. on TG vs EE bioavailability; Hilbert et al. (2015) Scientific Reports.

What to Actually Look For

When evaluating a fish oil supplement, these are the criteria that matter:

✓
Triglyceride form (rTG or natural TG) — not ethyl ester. This is the single most important factor.
✓
TOTOX value under 10 — this is the oxidation score. Some brands publish it; it's worth looking for.
✓
Molecular distillation — removes heavy metals and environmental contaminants.
✓
At least 1,000mg EPA+DHA per serving — read the small print, not the headline dose.
✓
Third-party testing — IFOS (International Fish Oil Standards) certification is the most rigorous independent standard.

Products that consistently meet these criteria in independent testing include Nordic Naturals Ultimate Omega, Wiley's Finest Wild Alaskan Fish Oil, and Carlson Labs Super Omega-3. All three are available through iHerb at substantially lower cost than NZ retail. They are not equivalent to supermarket fish oil — the quality difference is meaningful and well-documented.

One good fish oil capsule beats four cheap oxidised ones. Every time.
