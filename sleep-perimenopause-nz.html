It's 3am. You're not anxious about anything specific. You're not in pain. The room is quiet, your family is asleep, and your brain — inexplicably, maddeningly — is just on. Racing, or sometimes just softly, stubbornly awake. And you lie there, staring at the ceiling, knowing that tomorrow will be harder because of this, and that somehow even that knowledge isn't enough to make your brain let go.

Sleep is the reason I turned to VelaOra. Not hot flushes. Not brain fog. Not the anxiety. Sleep — or the loss of it — was what finally made me go looking for answers. Because if you've never experienced the particular misery of sustained sleep disruption, it's difficult to explain what it does to a person. The brain fog the next day isn't just tiredness — it's a specific kind of cognitive flatness where concentration slips, the world feels slightly too difficult, and emotions sit closer to the surface than usual. It's a nightmare. And if you'd been kept up by a child, or a worry, or something you could point to — you could at least make sense of it. But just lying awake for no discernible reason, in a body that used to sleep, is something else entirely.

I researched. I tried things. I found what worked and what didn't. And eventually I built something — but let's start with the science, because understanding why this happens is the first step to not feeling like you're losing your mind.

Why Perimenopause Steals Your Sleep

Sleep disruption in the years surrounding the final period isn't random, and it isn't weakness. It is a direct, physiological consequence of hormonal change — and once you understand the mechanisms, the 3am waking starts to make a different kind of sense.

Progesterone. This is the big one that doesn't get talked about enough. Progesterone is a natural sedative. It binds to GABA receptors in the brain — the same receptors targeted by sleep medications and benzodiazepines — and has a genuine, measurable calming effect on the nervous system. As progesterone declines in perimenopause, that buffer disappears. The brain's natural off-switch weakens. This is why so many women describe not just difficulty falling asleep, but a quality of wakefulness that feels wired — alert in a way that doesn't match how tired their body is.

Oestrogen and sleep architecture. Oestrogen plays a role in regulating the sleep cycle, particularly the amount of time spent in deep, restorative slow-wave sleep. As oestrogen declines, deep sleep decreases and you spend more time in lighter sleep stages — meaning smaller disturbances (a noise, a shift in temperature, a thought) are enough to wake you fully. You're sleeping less deeply and so you surface more easily.

Cortisol timing. In a well-regulated system, cortisol — your primary stress and waking hormone — peaks in the morning and is low at night. Perimenopause disrupts this pattern. Cortisol can spike at the wrong times, including in the early hours of the morning, which is one explanation for the extremely common phenomenon of waking between 3 and 4am feeling alert and unable to return to sleep. Your body has triggered a waking signal at precisely the wrong time.

Core body temperature. Sleep onset requires a drop in core body temperature. Hot flushes and night sweats — which affect approximately 75% of women in perimenopause — disrupt this process directly, waking you from sleep just as you've settled. Even women who don't experience obvious hot flushes can have subtle temperature dysregulation that fragments sleep without a clear event they can name.

The Numbers

Up to 47% of perimenopausal women report significant sleep disturbance — making it one of the most common symptoms, and one of the least frequently addressed in clinical settings. A 2018 review in Sleep Medicine Reviews found sleep disruption was significantly associated with declining ovarian function independent of hot flush frequency — meaning even women without obvious vasomotor symptoms experience poorer sleep as hormones shift.

The 3–4am waking pattern is so common it has been specifically studied. Research implicates both the cortisol peak irregularity and the transition out of REM sleep in the early morning — a point at which, without adequate progesterone to ease the nervous system back down, the brain catches on an anxious or active thought and can't let go.

References: Kravitz & Joffe (Sleep Med Rev, 2011); Baker et al. (J Clin Endocrinol Metab, 2012); Freeman et al. (Menopause, 2015).

What Doesn't Work

Before we get to what does work — a word on what doesn't, because many of us try these first.

Alcohol. A glass of wine to wind down is one of the most common sleep strategies and one of the most counterproductive. Alcohol does help you fall asleep faster — but it significantly fragments the second half of the night, suppresses REM sleep, and raises core body temperature. It makes the 3am wake-up worse, not better. If you're struggling with sleep and drinking in the evening, removing that variable is worth trying for two weeks before anything else.

Antihistamine sleep aids. Over-the-counter sleep aids (many contain diphenhydramine) work initially but tolerance develops within days. They also carry a next-day grogginess that compounds the cognitive effects of poor sleep rather than resolving them. Not a long-term solution.

Trying harder. The more effort you put into falling asleep, the more alert your brain becomes. This is called psychophysiological arousal — the brain learns to associate bed with wakefulness and the effort of trying to sleep, and the association strengthens over time. Lying in bed awake for extended periods actively makes insomnia worse. (This is why one of the CBT-I techniques below feels so counterintuitive — but works.)

Expensive apps with generic content. I tried several. They're beautifully designed and eye-wateringly priced, and most of the content — the meditations, the breathing exercises, the sleep stories — is not specific to what's happening in a perimenopausal or postmenopausal woman's nervous system. Generic relaxation content helps some people some of the time. It didn't help me enough.

Magnesium Glycinate — The Nighttime Mineral

Magnesium is involved in over 300 enzymatic reactions in the body, and among them are several that directly support sleep. It activates GABA receptors — the same receptors that progesterone used to support and that are now underactive. It supports melatonin production. It relaxes smooth muscle and reduces the physical tension that can keep the nervous system activated at night.

The form matters. Magnesium oxide (the most common form in cheap supplements) has poor bioavailability and is largely absorbed in the gut rather than crossing into the brain. Magnesium glycinate is chelated to the amino acid glycine — itself a calming neurotransmitter — and absorbs significantly better. It is the form most consistently used in the sleep research and the one least likely to cause digestive upset.

A 2012 double-blind RCT in the Journal of Research in Medical Sciences — specifically in older adults with insomnia — found that magnesium supplementation significantly improved sleep time, sleep efficiency, early morning waking, and serum melatonin levels versus placebo. A 2021 systematic review in BMC Complementary Medicine and Therapies confirmed the benefit across multiple studies.

The evidence-based dose is 300–400mg of elemental magnesium glycinate, taken 30–60 minutes before bed. Many women notice a difference within the first week. Some within nights.

Magnesium was the first thing that made a tangible difference to my sleep. Not dramatic — not a miracle — but a quieter nervous system at bedtime and fewer of those full-alert 3am awakenings. It takes the edge off in a way that nothing else had.

CBT-I — The Gold Standard Nobody Tells You About

Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Insomnia — CBT-I — is rated by the American College of Physicians and sleep researchers worldwide as the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia. Not melatonin. Not sleeping pills. CBT-I. In head-to-head trials, it outperforms medication, and crucially, its effects last — because it addresses the underlying patterns that maintain insomnia rather than masking symptoms.

It works on two levels. The behavioural component targets the habits and patterns that have built up around sleep — the lying awake in bed for hours, the irregular sleep schedule, the checking of the clock, the compensatory napping. The cognitive component targets the thoughts: the catastrophising about tomorrow ("I won't cope"), the anxiety about the sleep itself, the hypervigilance that turns the bedroom into a place associated with struggle rather than rest.

The core techniques:

◇
Sleep restriction. Counterintuitive but powerfully effective. You temporarily limit the time in bed to match the time you're actually sleeping — building sleep pressure and re-establishing the association between bed and sleep. It's uncomfortable for a week. The payoff is significant.
◇
Stimulus control. Bed is for sleep (and intimacy) only. No screens, no reading, no lying awake for extended periods. If you can't sleep after 20 minutes, you get up and do something calm in low light until you feel genuinely sleepy, then return. This breaks the learned association between bed and wakefulness.
◇
Cognitive restructuring. Identifying and challenging the thoughts that perpetuate insomnia. "I'll never sleep again" becomes "I've slept before and I'll sleep again — tonight may be hard but I can manage tomorrow." The catastrophising is usually worse than the reality.
◇
Relaxation techniques. 4-7-8 breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, body scans. Not as an alternative to CBT-I — as a tool within it. They lower physiological arousal in the moments before sleep and during night waking.

Traditional CBT-I requires a trained therapist and several sessions. That's not accessible for most people — especially at the price point most sleep therapists charge. Digital CBT-I (dCBT-I) programmes have strong evidence — a 2016 Lancet study found digital CBT-I significantly improved insomnia symptoms and was more effective than a sleep hygiene control. The problem, again, is cost and relevance. Most programmes are not designed for women navigating hormonal sleep disruption specifically.
