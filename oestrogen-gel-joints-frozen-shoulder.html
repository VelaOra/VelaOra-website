I'm Rubbing My Oestrogen Gel on My Achilles. Here's What the Science Says (and Doesn't)

S
Sarah at VelaOra
November 2026

My Achilles has been sore for two years. Not dramatic, just always there. It's stiff first thing, it complains on stairs, and it reminds me every time I try to get back into walking properly. I can't run without real pain, and football is out the window for now if I want to be able to walk the next day.

I've done everything right: a sports doctor, physio, months of loading exercises, high-dose omega-3. It's a bit better on good days, but it has never healed.

So when I came across the idea of applying oestrogen gel over a sore joint, I sat up. Dr Louise Newson has talked about women with frozen shoulders and stubborn joint pain whose symptoms eased once their HRT was right. It made me wonder: what if I put some of my usual gel near the joint that's been bothering me for two years?

I've been trying it for two weeks now. Here's what the science says, and how it's going.

Why Oestrogen and Joints Might Be Linked

Tendons, ligaments and joint capsules all have oestrogen receptors. Oestrogen helps with collagen turnover, joint lubrication and keeping inflammation in check. As levels fall in perimenopause, many of us notice the aches arrive: stiff hands, sore shoulders, heel pain.

More than 70% of women in menopause report musculoskeletal symptoms. Frozen shoulder is up to four times more common in women than men, and it peaks between 40 and 60. Recovery takes an average of around two and a half years, so it's no wonder so many of us are looking for anything that helps.

What the Research Actually Shows

Frozen shoulder. A Duke University study of 1,952 women aged 45 to 60 found that women not on HRT had higher odds of frozen shoulder than those on HRT. But only 152 women were on HRT, and the result wasn't statistically significant. The researchers called it a starting point and plan to study a larger group.

Tendons. The evidence is mixed. A review of HRT for tendinopathy found only one trial (in hip tendon pain) and couldn't recommend HRT to improve outcomes. A large 2026 US database study actually found slightly more tendon problems in women on HRT. That's observational, so it can't tell us why.

Applying gel directly to the joint. I couldn't find any published trials. Dr Newson's clinic reports that many patients feel their joints improve on the right dose of HRT. I looked through what other menopause doctors and specialists are saying too, from Harvard to Duke to Yale. They all talk about HRT working through your whole body, whether it's a patch, gel or spray, and none of them recommend rubbing it onto a specific joint. Moving gel to a sore spot is a logical idea, but for now it's still anecdotal.

The Honest Summary

There's a plausible biological reason oestrogen could matter for joints and tendons, and a small early signal for frozen shoulder. There is no trial yet showing that rubbing gel onto a particular joint works better than applying it in the usual place.

References: Ford, Wittstein et al., Duke University (retrospective cohort, 1,952 women); Dr Louise Newson, "Frozen shoulder and menopause: is there a link?" (Newson Health, 2024); Curtis & Edwards, Keele University critically appraised topic on HRT and tendinopathy (2024); Bcharah et al., AAOS 2026 abstract on HRT and tendon injury.

What I'm Doing, and What I'm Not

What I'm doing is simple. I'm using my usual prescribed dose and just applying some of it over the area around my Achilles instead of my thigh. Gel is absorbed into your bloodstream wherever you put it, so it's not extra oestrogen. Whether putting it near a sore joint gives a local boost is the part nobody has tested yet.

What I'm not doing is adding more gel on top of my dose.

If you want to try this:

✓
Have a chat with whoever prescribes your HRT first.
✓
Check your product information on where it can go.
✓
Avoid broken or irritated skin, and wash your hands afterwards.
✓
Be careful about skin-to-skin contact with partners, kids and pets while it dries.

Two Weeks In

So far, it's promising. But I'll be honest: it's hard to pinpoint exactly why. My Achilles has always been up and down when it comes to soreness, so a good few days could be the gel, or could just be a good few days. I guess time will tell.

I'm optimistic. I'll keep going and report back honestly, whether it works or not. I'm hoping for a miracle, but I'll settle for walking down the stairs without wincing.

And I'll let you know if I'm back on the field any time soon.
