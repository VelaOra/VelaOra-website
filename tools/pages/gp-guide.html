Three doctors. All women. Not one of them said the word. This guide is everything I wish I had known before I walked into those appointments.

I have sat in GP offices in New Zealand and described symptoms that, taken together, form a textbook perimenopause picture — and left with referrals for ophthalmology, suggestions about stress management, and not one mention of hormones. A national survey found that 89% of NZ women aged 40–60 have experienced symptoms associated with menopause, and the majority are being managed in pieces rather than as a whole.

This guide gives you the exact language, the right questions, and a clear plan for what to do when you are not heard. Print it. Save it. Take it with you.

Before Your Appointment: Build Your Case

Your GP has approximately 15 minutes with you. That is not long enough to piece together a hormonal picture unless you arrive with the work already done. Here is what to prepare:

Track These for 2–4 Weeks Before Your Appointment

  • Sleep — what time you wake, how often, whether you feel hot
  • Mood — anxiety, irritability, low mood, and what time of day or month they occur
  • Cognitive symptoms — brain fog, word retrieval, concentration
  • Physical symptoms — joint pain, dry eyes, itchy skin or ears, palpitations, headaches
  • Cycle changes — longer, shorter, heavier, lighter, or skipped periods
  • Energy — when you feel lowest and whether it follows a pattern
  • Libido changes

Bring this written down. Doctors respond to documented patterns far more readily than to verbal descriptions recalled on the spot.

In the Appointment: What to Say

The language you use matters. Vague descriptions invite vague responses. Here are the phrases that work:

Use These Exact Words

"I'd like to discuss perimenopause."
Name it. Not "I've been feeling off." Not "I think I might be going through something." Use the word.

"I've been tracking my symptoms for [X weeks] and I'd like to go through them with you."
This signals you are organised and informed. Hand over your written list.

"I understand that blood tests may not be conclusive, but I'd like to discuss treatment options based on my symptoms."
This prevents the conversation being closed down by a normal FSH result.

"Can you refer me to a menopause specialist, or is there someone in your practice with a particular interest in women's hormonal health?"
GPs vary enormously in their menopause knowledge. Asking for a specialist is not being difficult — it is good healthcare.

Tests Worth Asking For

A perimenopause diagnosis in women over 45 should be made on symptoms and history — the NICE guidelines, which NZ GPs increasingly follow, are clear on this. Blood tests can still be useful to rule out other causes and establish a baseline.

Ask About These

  • FSH and LH — with the caveat that a normal result does not rule out perimenopause
  • Oestradiol — a single snapshot, useful as a baseline
  • Thyroid function (TSH, T3, T4) — thyroid issues mimic many perimenopause symptoms
  • Full blood count — anaemia explains fatigue and brain fog
  • Vitamin D — deficiency is very common in NZ and worsens mood, joint pain, and fatigue
  • Iron studies — especially if you have heavy periods
  • Testosterone — if you have low libido, low energy, or cognitive symptoms, ask for this specifically. Many GPs will not offer it without prompting.

A normal blood test does not rule out perimenopause. Your symptoms are data. Trust them.

Asking About HRT in New Zealand

Hormone replacement therapy — now more often called MHT, menopausal hormone therapy — has been significantly rehabilitated by the evidence over the past decade. Body-identical HRT using transdermal oestrogen and micronised progesterone carries a much lower clot and breast cancer risk than older oral synthetic formulations. It is available in New Zealand, though prescribing confidence varies between GPs.

What to Ask

  • "Are you familiar with the NICE menopause guidelines?" — this orients the conversation
  • "What are my options for body-identical or transdermal HRT?"
  • "Can we discuss testosterone? I understand it is prescribed for women in Australia and the UK — is that something you can support?"
  • "If HRT is not something you are comfortable prescribing, can you refer me to someone who is?"

Testosterone for women — used for libido, energy, and cognitive symptoms — is not formally approved for women in NZ but is legally prescribable off-label. Some GPs will not prescribe it simply because they have not been trained in it. This is a knowledge gap, not a safety issue.

If You Are Dismissed

If you leave an appointment having been told your bloods are normal, advised to try antidepressants, or sent away without your symptoms being considered as a whole — you have options.

Your Next Steps

  • Request a longer appointment specifically to discuss perimenopause and treatment options
  • Ask for a different GP within the same practice with an interest in women's health
  • Request a referral to a gynaecologist — you are entitled to ask for this
  • Consider telehealth — several NZ telehealth services have GPs with stronger menopause training
  • Australasian Menopause Society — menopause.org.au has a "find a menopause doctor" directory that includes NZ clinicians

You are not being difficult. You are not catastrophising. You are a woman whose body is going through a significant hormonal shift, and you deserve a GP who can meet you there. If yours cannot, find one who can.

Find a doctor who listens. If you leave feeling dismissed, you are entitled to seek another opinion. It is not being difficult. It is advocating for your health.

NZ Resources

  • Healthify NZ — healthify.nz
  • Family Planning NZ — hormonal health consultations in some regions
  • Jean Hailes for Women's Health — jeanhailes.org.au — Australian but highly evidence-based and widely used in NZ
